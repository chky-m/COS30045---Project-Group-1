Hospitalised injuries from road crashes in Australia, 2011–2021

Who ends up in hospital?

Road-crash hospitalisations rose by about one-sixth between 2011 and 2021, then levelled off after 2017. The burden is uneven: who gets hurt, how badly, and where they live each tell a different story. National totals hide state stories, some of which are partly reporting changes, and First Nations hospitalisations grew far faster than the rest.

Overview

Why this matters

Hospitalised injuries from road crashes are a major, ongoing public-health and economic burden. Between 2011 and 2021, Australian hospitals recorded road-crash hospitalisations, with large differences by state and territory, road user, age, sex and First Nations status. That information usually sits inside large, multi-sheet spreadsheets; this story makes the patterns visible and comparable.

Audience

Policymakers & agencies

BITRE and state and territory transport and health agencies.

Road-safety researchers

Researchers who need comparable, documented views of the BITRE data.

General public

Anyone interested in road-trauma trends in Australia.

Purpose

  • Prioritise: show where the hospitalised-injury burden is concentrated (by road user, age group, region and population) to help target interventions.
  • Inform: raise public awareness of road-trauma trends and of the groups most affected.
  • Be transparent: keep the data's limits in view (reporting changes, unpublished values and the missing post-June-2020 death data) so the figures are read responsibly.

Dataset 1 · National

The national picture

How big is the problem, and who is hurt worst?

Australian hospitals now record close to forty thousand road-crash hospitalisations a year, up from about 34,000 in 2011. The series grew through the mid-2010s and then levelled off, and two states changed how they count emergency-department-only patients along the way. Behind the total, volume and severity point at different people: car occupants and riders fill the wards, while pedestrians and people aged 75 and over make up a far larger share of deaths in hospital than of admissions.

Dataset 3 · First Nations

First Nations and non-Indigenous people

How do First Nations people's road-crash hospitalisations differ?

First Nations people's road-crash hospitalisations rose much faster than non-Indigenous people's between 2011 and 2021, and they look different: younger, more often from remote areas, and more often involving motorcycles and non-collision crashes. These charts describe counts and the mix of hospitalisations, not injury risk; differences also reflect population size and age structure, where people live, and how First Nations status is recorded.

So what does all this tell us?

Takeaways

What the data tells us

  1. Hospitalisations rose, then flattened. Up from 2011 to 2021, almost all of it before 2017; 2018–2021 was flat ().

  2. Cyclists are the fastest-growing group, and rose in every state and territory.

  3. 7% → 26%

    Pedestrians are 7% of cases but 26% of deaths in hospital; people aged 75+ are 6% of cases but 29% (2011–2019).

  4. to

    State trends differ widely. Since 2018, a window with no counting-rule changes, growth runs from Queensland to NSW. NSW's and Victoria's decade figures partly reflect reporting changes.

  5. First Nations hospitalisations nearly doubled, against for non-Indigenous people; First Nations patients are younger and far more often live in remote areas.

What this data can't tell us

  • No population or exposure rates. Our files have no population, licence or distance-travelled denominators, so nothing here says one group or state is "riskier" per person.
  • Deaths are only those in hospital, within 30 days of admission, from the national dataset, and only to June 2020. Deaths at the crash scene are outside this data.
  • The datasets don't add up to each other. The eight states fall – short of the national total each year, and the First Nations workbook excludes cases with unstated status.
  • Category schemes differ between workbooks, so road-user, counterparty, age and remoteness groups cannot be compared directly across datasets.
  • Causes. COVID-19 restrictions, infrastructure or policy are context only; the data shows when things changed, not why.

Methods

Data and methods

BITRE, Hospitalised injuries from road crashes (AIHW National Hospital Morbidity Database, September 2023 release): the national monthly file (Cause of injury = Traffic), the State & Territory workbook (Tables 1–5, six-monthly) and the First Nations workbook (six-monthly). All three were cleaned in KNIME; unpublished (n.p.) values are kept as missing, never zero. The figures on this page are generated from the processed CSVs by Website/scripts/build_data.py.

Series breaks and soft breaks

  • Jul 2012, Victoria: emergency-department-only cases excluded from 1 July 2012 (dictionary estimate: −2.8% on the 2012 national count).
  • Jun 2017, NSW: ED-only episodes no longer counted as admissions; injury categories are disproportionately affected.
  • 1 Jul 2015, bed days: inclusion rules changed for transfers and Z50 rehabilitation (2015–16 financial year). Bed days are never compared across this date.
  • Jul 2020, national: Died_cases and Died_bed_days end; Count of cases is incomplete. Hospitalisations are used for every time series, and July 2020 is a soft break.
  • ICD-10-AM: the classification edition changes every two years, so minor coding drift is possible.

National vs sum of states

The states are never stacked to make the national total. The national figure is higher every year; the workbook notes that national totals include patients whose residence was unrecorded or migratory/offshore, which is the most likely explanation, though not an exact match.
